      Does it actually work?

      We tried to fool ourselves. Here's what happened.

      We stood in 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010 and 2014 and made 10-year forecasts using only data available at the time. Then we compared them with what really happened: about 1,000 forecasts per outcome. Lower error is better.

      How a forecast is made

      🛰️
      1 · Pull

      17 indicators × 217 economies × 1970–2025, straight from the World Bank Indicators API.

      🧬
      2 · Fingerprint

      Each country-year becomes 18 z-scores covering income, economic structure, health, demography, education, size and momentum.

      🔎
      3 · Match

      A weighted distance to about 12,000 past country-years. We keep each country's best match and take the 12 closest.

      👻
      4 · Replay

      Their real next decades, corrected for each era's global shocks, become the forecast range.

      Scorecard

      The fine print

      Terms & Conditions of Forecasting

      1. Data. Every number is pulled by a Python pipeline from api.worldbank.org/v2/country/all/indicator/{code}, following the pagination. We drop regional aggregates using /v2/country metadata and interpolate small gaps within each country.
      2. Fingerprint. Each country-year is 18 z-scores covering income, economic structure, health, demography, education, size and momentum. Skewed indicators are logged. Income counts double, and every other category counts equally.
      3. No time-bound tech. Mobile phones and the internet are left out, so that 1985 and 2025 can be compared fairly.
      4. Matching. Weighted RMS distance over the indicators both country-years report, with at least 60% of the weight overlapping. We keep each country's single best year and take the 12 closest, with Gaussian kernel weights (bandwidth = median distance).
      5. Era correction. A 1981 look-alike then lived through the 1980s debt crisis. So we measure each look-alike's growth against the world median in its own decade, then add back the long-run average. This is analog forecasting (Lorenz, 1969), a technique from meteorology applied to development data.
      6. No peeking. In the backtest, the pool of look-alikes, the z-score scaling and every baseline's training data only use what was known at each origin year.
      7. Side effects may include humility. History rhymes rather than repeats. Look-alikes capture structure, not policy, conflict or luck. Our 80% ranges contain the truth about 70–78% of the time, so they are a bit too narrow.
      8. Not financial or policy advice. The What-if page shows whose history a scenario resembles, not what causes growth.

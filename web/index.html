      Backtest

      Does it work?

      We pretended it was 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2014, made ten-year forecasts using only the data available at the time, and checked them against what actually happened. That's about 1,000 forecasts per outcome.

      How it works

      1
      Pull the data

      17 indicators for 217 economies from 1970 to 2025, from the World Bank Indicators API.

      2
      Describe each year

      Every country-year becomes 18 standardized numbers: income, economic structure, health, population, schooling, size, and recent growth.

      3
      Find the twins

      Measure the distance to every earlier country-year, keep each country's best match, and take the 12 closest.

      4
      Replay their decade

      The twins' actual next ten years, adjusted for what the whole world went through at the time, become the forecast range.

      Results

      Why nearest neighbors

      Every forecast shows its work

      The model is k-nearest neighbors, a basic machine learning method, run across time instead of across people or products. We picked it because every forecast comes with its reasons: the twelve real countries it's based on. If someone at a finance ministry or an aid agency thinks Korea in 2002 is a bad comparison for their country, they can see that and discount the forecast. With a black-box model they'd have to take the number on trust. We didn't give up accuracy for this either. In the backtest, the twins plus a simple regression beat the regression alone on all four outcomes.

      Who could use it

      • Planners and NGOs looking for countries that were in the same spot and what those countries did.
      • Journalists checking whether a government's growth target has ever been hit from a similar starting point.
      • Students learning development economics, who can see the textbook cases (Korea, Botswana, Poland) as real data.

      Method notes

      1. Data. A Python pipeline pulls every indicator from api.worldbank.org/v2/country/all/indicator/{code} and follows the pagination. Regional aggregates are dropped using the /v2/country metadata, and short gaps are filled in within each country.
      2. Features. 18 standardized values per country-year. Skewed ones (GDP, population, child mortality, trade) are logged first. Income counts double in the distance, and each other category counts the same.
      3. Left out on purpose. Mobile phones, internet use, and anything else that only exists in recent decades. Otherwise every country today would just match another country today.
      4. Matching. Weighted RMS distance over the indicators both years report, with at least 60% of the weight overlapping. We keep each country's single closest year, take the 12 closest countries, and weight them with a Gaussian kernel.
      5. Era adjustment. A twin from 1981 went straight into the 1980s debt crisis. We measure each twin's growth relative to the world median over the same years, then add back the long-run average. The idea comes from analog weather forecasting (Lorenz, 1969).
      6. No peeking. In the backtest, the twin pool, the standardization, and every baseline model only use data from before each starting year.
      7. Limits. Twins capture where a country is, not its politics, wars, or luck. The 80% ranges contain the real outcome 70 to 78% of the time, so they're somewhat too narrow.
      8. What if and lessons. Both show correlations in historical data. Neither tells you what causes growth.
